## 1. DESCRIBE IMAGE Templates

### Template A: Data Charts - Single Category (Bar/Line/Pie/Table/Map)
**Transition Style: Comparison Flow**

```
This [chart type] shows information about [title].

At the top, [largest item] has the highest number, which is around [max value].

Coming in next, [second item] shows about [second value].

On the other end, [smallest item] has the lowest at around [min value].

Worth noting, both [other item 1] and [other item 2] carry weight here too.

In summary, this chart presents real data about [title].
```

### Template B: Data Charts - Multiple Categories
**Transition Style: Category Switching**

```
This [chart type] gives information about [title].

When we check [category 1], [cat1 max item] leads at around [cat1 max value], while [cat1 min item] sits lowest at about [cat1 min value].

Switching to [category 2], [cat2 max item] tops this group at around [cat2 max value].

As for other data, [any item 1] and [any item 2] also play a role in this chart.

Overall, the data compares different groups about [title].
```

### Template C: Flow Charts/Process Diagrams
**Transition Style: Sequential Movement**

```
This flow chart shows the process of [title].

Everything begins with [step 1] as the starting point.

Moving forward, the next stage involves [step 2].

Continuing on, [step 3] comes into play.

To wrap things up, [last step] marks the final stage.

In summary, these steps show how [title] works.
```

*For循环图: Replace last sentence with: "Once finished, everything loops back to the beginning."*

### Template D: General Images/Photos
**Transition Style: Spatial Exploration**

```
This picture shows information about [title].

Right in [area 1/position 1], ___ stands out as the main focus.

Just beside that, ___ and ___ add important context.

If we shift to [area 2/position 2], ___ and ___ bring in more details.

Scattered around, ___ and ___ fill out the scene as well.

Overall, these elements work together to present [title].
```

### Template E: Maps/Geographic Images
**Transition Style: Directional Guide**

```
This map gives details about [title].

Starting from the top, [location 1] appears with [detail].

Heading to the center, [location 2] shows up with [detail].

Down at the bottom, [location 3] sits at around [detail].

Looking at key spots, [feature 1] and [feature 2] mark important locations.

In total, this map displays the layout of [title].
```

## 2. RETELL LECTURE Template

```
This lecture mainly discusses [topic].

First, the lecture starts by introducing [point] and [point].

Second, [point] and [point] are mentioned, which connects to the topic.

Third, the speaker gives many details about [point] and [point].

Also, the lecture discusses [point] and [point] to support the main idea.

Overall, the lecture covers the topic of [topic].
```

**Tips:**
- Note down "phrases" not just single words
- If you have complete sentences, paraphrase them
- Each content sentence has 2 blanks - combine points with "and" if needed
- Repeat points if you don't have enough content

## 4. RESPOND TO A SITUATION Template

### Opening (1-2 sentences)
**Known recipient:** Hi / Excuse me, [name].
**Unknown recipient:** Hi, excuse me! / Hi, how are you?
**Phone message:** Hi, this is [your name].

*For formal contexts:* Hope I'm not interrupting! / May I have a moment?

### Body (2-3 sentences)
Paraphrase the situation in first person, add relevant details

### Closing (1-2 sentences)

**Making suggestions:**
- Informal: I think it's a good idea if you/we can...
- Formal: I suggest we consider...

**Seeking advice:**
- Informal: What do you think about...?
- Formal: Could you provide some guidance on...?

**Seeking help:**
- Informal: Would you help me with...? Thanks so much!
- Formal: Would it be possible for you/me to...? I'd appreciate your help.

**Apologizing:**
- Informal: Sorry about that!
- Formal: I sincerely apologize for...

**Thanking:**
- Informal: Thank you for...!
- Formal: I'm deeply grateful for your...

## 5. SUMMARIZE SPOKEN TEXT Template (50-70 words)

### Full Template (28 words base)

```
The presentation covers [topic]. To begin with, the speaker explained that [key point]. Next, it was stated that [key point]. Furthermore, the lecturer pointed out that [key point]. Finally, the discussion revealed that [key point].
```

### Simplified Template (12 words base)
*For students who can capture more content*

```
The lecture discusses the topic of [topic]. First, [key point]. Second, [key point]. In addition/However, [key point]. Moreover, [key point].
```

### Minimal Template (8 words base) ⚡
*Absolute minimum - fastest to write*

```
The lecture discusses [TOPIC NAME]. First, the speaker mentioned [KEY POINT 1]. Second, [KEY POINT 2]. Moreover, [KEY POINT 3].
```

**Alternative connectors:**
- First → "The speaker mentioned" / "The lecturer explained" / "It was stated that"
- Second → "Additionally" / "Furthermore" / "The speaker also mentioned"
- Moreover → "Finally" / "Lastly" / "In conclusion"

**Strategy:**
- **3 key points minimum** (50% of content is enough)
- **Mix short and long sentences** for natural flow
- **8-word base + 3 key points** = ~55-65 words total
- **Fast to memorize** and write under time pressure

**Example:**
> The lecture discusses climate change. First, the speaker mentioned global temperatures are rising rapidly. Second, polar ice caps are melting at an alarming rate. Moreover, extreme weather events are becoming more frequent.

**Note:** Must be written as one continuous paragraph (no line breaks)

## 6. SUMMARIZE WRITTEN TEXT Template (5-75 words)

```
______, [connector] _____, [connector] ______, [connector] ______, [connector] ______.
```

### Common Connectors:
- **Parallel:** and
- **Contrast:** but, whereas
- **Concession:** although
- **Cause:** because
- **Result:** so
- **Accompanying:** while
- **Explanation:** which means

**Tips:**
- Select key sentences from each section
- Focus on first/last sentences and logical markers
- Paraphrase the original text
- Use commas and connectors (only ONE period at the very end)
- Must be one complete sentence within 5-75 words

## 7. WRITE ESSAY Templates (200-300 words)

**✅ PROVEN APPROACH - November 2025 Testing:**
Template B achieved **26/26 perfect score** when used correctly with the balanced approach below.

**🎯 KEY SUCCESS FACTORS:**

1. **Reuse Topic Keywords** - Extract and repeat 5-10 keywords from the prompt throughout your essay
2. **Moderate Vocabulary** - Use intermediate-level academic words, not overly complex or too simple
3. **Realistic Examples** - Mention real scenarios, plausible situations (avoid fake statistics when possible)
4. **Clear Structure** - Follow template paragraphs strictly (intro → advantage → challenge → conclusion)
5. **Balance Content** - 60-70% from topic keywords + your simple reasoning, 30-40% template structure

**⚠️ IMPORTANT NOTES:**
- Different practice platforms may score differently (PTE Magic vs APEUni showed significant variance in testing)
- Template phrases work BEST when combined with topic-specific vocabulary
- Don't make vocabulary too simple ("better", "good") - use moderate academic words
- Provide specific examples rather than generic claims

## Balanced Approach: Moderate Vocabulary Strategy (RECOMMENDED)

**🎯 26/26 Perfect Score Strategy:**

### The Winning Formula:
1. **Extract 5-10 keywords from topic** → Use them throughout your essay
2. **Add moderate academic vocabulary** → Not too simple, not too complex
3. **Follow Template B structure strictly** → 4 paragraphs exactly as written
4. **Use realistic examples** → Real companies, plausible scenarios, university research

### Moderate Vocabulary Bank (Use These Instead of Too-Simple Words)

**Instead of "better" use:**
- valuable, significant, improved, enhanced, beneficial

**Instead of "important" use:**
- compelling, crucial, essential, vital, substantial

**Instead of "help" use:**
- facilitate, foster, support, promote, enable

**Instead of "make" use:**
- generate, create, produce, cause, lead to

**Instead of "show" use:**
- demonstrate, reveal, indicate, illustrate, present

**Instead of "problem" use:**
- challenge, issue, difficulty, concern, obstacle

**Instead of "good" use:**
- effective, successful, productive, positive, advantageous

**Instead of "understanding" use:**
- awareness, comprehension, knowledge, insight, perspective

**Academic Transition Phrases (Use Naturally):**
- "To begin with" / "Initially" / "First of all"
- "In particular" / "Specifically" / "For instance"
- "However" / "Nevertheless" / "On the other hand"
- "Ultimately leading to" / "Consequently" / "As a result"
- "Taken together" / "Overall" / "In conclusion"

**Realistic Example Sources (No Fake Statistics):**
- "university research reveals that..."
- "educational institutions demonstrate that..."
- "workplace studies show that..."
- "evidence from [field] suggests that..."
- "companies like [real company] have successfully..."
- "research indicates that many [subject]..."

**Avoid:**
❌ "80% of companies" or "60% of organizations" (unless from topic)
❌ "annual report of [generic source]"
❌ Too simple: "very good", "very bad", "help a lot"
❌ Too complex: "paradigm", "multifaceted", "synergistic"

### Example: Balanced Approach (26/26 Score)

**Topic:** Travel in education - is it necessary?

**Perfect Score Essay (272 words):**

In recent years, the topic of travel in education has emerged as a prominent subject of public and academic discussion. I contend that travel is a valuable component of quality education even though some scholars achieve success without leaving home, and the following analysis presents the primary arguments supporting this viewpoint.

To begin with, one compelling advantage of educational travel is its potential to facilitate practical learning and cultural awareness. In particular, visiting different places can foster direct observation of historical sites and diverse societies, ultimately leading to broader outcomes such as deeper understanding and memorable learning experiences. For instance, evidence from university research reveals that approximately 80% of students who traveled abroad experienced improved language skills and cultural knowledge as a direct result of real-world exposure.

However, it is equally important to acknowledge that staying home for education may also give rise to certain valid arguments. This is largely because dedicated scholars with books and digital resources are likely to generate deep knowledge through focused study, which may subsequently cause people to view travel as less necessary than hard work. According to research by educational institutions, over 60% of successful scholars have been able to achieve academic excellence without extensive travel stemming from disciplined reading and consistent effort.

Taken together, these considerations demonstrate that both approaches have merit, but travel adds unique experiential value to education, as the influence of real-world experiences and cross-cultural exposure is substantial and far-reaching. Thus, it is imperative for students and educational programs to dedicate appropriate time and resources to managing this issue effectively.

**What made this perfect:**
✅ Reused topic words: "travel", "education", "scholar", "home", "quality", "valuable"
✅ Moderate vocabulary: "facilitate", "cultural awareness", "dedicated scholars", "disciplined reading"
✅ Specific examples: "historical sites", "language skills", "focused study"
✅ Template B structure followed exactly
✅ Realistic sources: "university research", "educational institutions"
✅ Balanced argument addressing both sides
